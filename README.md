# Trees for Public Health *(in progress)*

## GENESIS

This summary explains how the City of Chicago's [Our Roots Chicago](https://www.chicago.gov/city/en/sites/our-roots-chicago/home.html) went from idea to practice. On June 6, 2019, I was invited to a workshop to discuss trees and their beneficial effects to health by The Nature Conservancy of Illinois at the [2019 Pritzker Forum on Global Cities](https://globalaffairs.org/events/2019-pritzker-forum-global-cities) Workshop "Nature for Urban Health” at the Peggy Notebaert Nature Museum. Chatham House Rules were in effect so I can't reveal the identity of the participants or what was said to encourage open and honest discussions. It was where the spark was lit to work on trees within the Chicago Department of Public Health, when I was the Director of Innovation at the time. And then on July 9, 2019, I attended CRTI's Lydia Scott's presentation at Chi Hack Night on [“Using Urban Forestry Data to Improve Health and Quality of Life in the Chicago Region.”](https://chihacknight.org/events/2019/07/09/chicago-region-trees-initiative) I approached Lydia and we began fruitful conversations around how trees and public health data can be used together to improve community health.

This repo was then created on July 19, 2019, to document evidence for developing a public health strategy to improve Chicago's urban canopy using a health equity lens to equitably plant new trees driven by community, maintaining and preserving  existing trees, improving tree benefit communications, and providing workforce development and job training. At first, this was a scan of the environment for evidence-based research, resources, best-practices, etc. It also served to accelerate the latest science and innovative progamming to promote alignment and unification of ad hoc and disparate tree initiatives across all stakeholders into a cohesive, coordinated approach to tree planting and sustainability of Chicago’s urban canopy by prioritizing and working together with historically marginalized, under- and dis-invested communities.

I began an [Openlands' TreeKeepers](https://openlands.org/programs/treekeepers-certification-and-program) certification that I completed on December 7, 2019 (I later became a member of Openlands' Board of Directors in 2021). On behalf of CDPH, I was later invited to join CRTI’s Executive Advisory Council in March 2020 and we began to collaborate with their GIS Administrator, Lindsay Darling around their LiDAR-based land cover data that was showing less trees in historically marginalized and underserved communities. Unfortunately, the COVID pandemic delayed a lot of the tree initiative planning since I was detailed by the City to supervise the public health tech modernization in 2020.

Prior to this, we were working on Phase 1 of [The Partnership for Healthy Cities](https://cities4health.org) award that was applied for by CDPH in 2017 and funded the Center for Spatial Data Science at University of Chicago to collect and visualize baseline air quality data for the City of Chicago from NASA satellites, EPA sensor networks, USGS, and NOAA climate data. In Phase 2, we added trees to mapping air quality in 2020 after pitching the ideas of using a map-based tool that can help prioritize communities and allocate trees and resources internally to CDPH and the Mayor's Office, and then to CRTI and USFS in December 2019. CRTI's Tree Canopy data was added to public health, environmental, social and economic data to better understand tree canopy in vulnerable neighborhoods throughout the city and to reduce the impacts of climate change on these communities. The Center for Spatial Data Science created an Environmental Tool that was piloted from late 2021 to 2022.


## PLAN

To draft a tree equity plan, I first applied CDC's [One Health](https://www.cdc.gov/one-health/about/index.html) as a framework that reveals how the health of humans, animals, and the environment are inextricably connected, emphasizing that vulnerability in one impacts the others. Basically, if one is vulnerable, we are all vulnerable. When you widen the justification of the framework, people can then decide for themselves the priorities they become involved with, whether it's biodiversity, shade, beautification, air quality, stormwater runoff, etc. Design it wide enough for anyone to practice as narrow as they want. 

To guide the framework, I used CDPH's Healthy Chicago 2025 to advance racial equity to close Chicago’s life expectancy gap to frame a tree equity strategy using public health indicators, under the section “Further the Health and Vibrancy of Neighborhoods” where all communities should have equitable access to environments that promote optimal health and well-being.

To justify CDPH involvement in tree planting to further the health of neighborhoods, the City of Chicago had adopted a “health in all policies” approach to make sure that the government works with communities to shape our social, economic, and physical environments in ways that promote health and racial equity.

Finally, Our Roots Chicago had to ensure equity and grounded in budget, plans, policies and programs that align, especially since the funding is through Chicago’s first-ever social bond issuance. The following are what finally guided Our Roots Chicago:

* **Chicago Climate Bond -** *$173M social bond (the first time the City of Chicago issued one) to invest in meaningful, substantive, and justice-oriented climate projects that will provide the City’s underserved communities with resilient infrastructure and green workforce development opportunities. <ins>$46 million for equity tree planting (75,000 trees in 5 years)</ins>.[(1)](https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/sites/climate-action-plan/documents/bond_info.pdf)*
      
* **Chicago Budget Equity -** *CDPH’s Environment, Climate, & Energy Outcomes inform decision making in policy areas such as land use/zoning, permitting, and enforcement to promote environmental justice through a cumulative impact assessment, including tree canopy cover.[(2)](https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/depts/obm/supp_info/budget-equity/Budget-Equity-Tool-Final.pdf)*
      
* **Chicago 2022 Climate Action Plan -** *Comprehensive plan to reduce the city’s GHG emissions 62% by 2040, anchored in values of economic inclusion and savings, pollution burden reduction, and equitable access to critical infrastructure and community health and resilience.[(3)](https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/sites/climate-action-plan/documents/Chicago-CAP-071822.pdf)*
      
* **Healthy Chicago 2025 Plan -** *CDPH’s Community Health Improvement Plan required by the Public Health Accreditation Board that focuses on racial and health equity to meet our goal of reducing the Black-white life expectancy gap including Environment as one of the priority areas in Priority Populations that include Black Chicagoans; Latino Chicagoans; Low-income Chicagoans; Communities disproportionately burdened by pollution; and Disinvested and gentrifying communities.[(4)](https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/depts/cdph/statistics_and_reports/HC2025_917_FINAL.pdf)*

* **CDPH Health Equity in All Policies Program -** *The City has adopted a "health in all policies" approach to make sure that the whole of government works together with community to shape our social, economic and physical environments in ways that promote health and racial equity.[(5)](https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/depts/cdph/supp_info/HealthEquityinAllPolicies_07202022_Final.pdf)*

* **Environmental Justice Executive Order -** *Advancing the City’s commitment to environmental justice for all Chicagoans, making consideration of health and other community impacts a priority across departments and in City policies.[(6)](https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/depts/cdph/environment/CoC_Executive-Order-2023-3.pdf)*

## TREE EQUITY WORKING GROUP

While the data clearly shows tree inequity and disparities in urban canopies across the US, this is not lost on our frontline communities that already disproportionately bear the brunt of environmental hazards and climate change impacts. As a result, they may become more vulnerable to the risks of extreme heat and urban heat islands, flooding, air pollution, as well as a loss of the health and climate co-benefits that trees provide. The latest research by CRTI shows that Chicago has one of the smallest urban canopies of any big city in the US, based on 2010 data from the [Chicago Region Trees Initiative Priority Map](https://mortonarb.maps.arcgis.com/apps/View/index.html?appid=0700702eb8fa4c31a4d957d71a09d690).

Prioritizing communities based on sociodemographic, health, air quality, climate, and tree canopy data may also help community building, workforce "green corps" training, development, and jobs, as well as tree plantings and preservation. Several Chicago community organizations already have existing plans for their neighborhood's urban canopy. We wanted to learn from their successes, promote and fund them, but not to replace their plans.

Through discussions with local and national partners, the data on tree census and estimation of the canopy density and heights using Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR), new tools that may better assist in the determination of neighborhoods with reduced urban canopies that may also have greater needs due to higher chronic cardiovascular and pulmonary diseases, high economic hardship, and low air quality. However, data is only as good as when it was last taken and sometimes it's not updated often or nonexistent. We used to state that this was a data-driven initiative because you can't manage what you don't measure, but no matter how great the latest data and tech innovations are, this initiative has become community-driven and data-informed. We initially ground truthed the data, but what really made a difference was residents and not data, who know where trees are and aren't in real time.

This is how the [Tree Equity Working Group](https://www.chicago.gov/city/en/sites/our-roots-chicago/home/tree-equity.html#:~:text=Community%20Tree%20Equity%20Working%20Group) (TEWG) was created in August 2021 when we had about 20 people join the first meeting of Our Roots, to co-create and co-develop with community, researchers and government - together. Not for communities, but with communities. The TEWG is now close to 200 people from 50 organizations, and they have been meeting ever since, open to the public, on the second Tuesday of every month for the last four years without one meeting being cancelled while I ran the meetings. The TEWG helped design the Our Roots logo, requested that we create a City website to help inform the public about misconceptions of trees, and later as a resource for promoting trees. They also helped desgined the website too, including its content, marketing materials, and direction and goals of the initiative with the whole of government present at every meeting. It was a unique opportunity to come face to face in regular discussions with communities, researchers and government on trees.


## TREE AMBASSADOR PROGRAM

This initiative could only be accomplished by working together with residents, many already maintain some of their neighborhood trees, and working with them to protect and restore their community trees in the way they wanted - in their culture, language, art, and events. We also wanted to lower the barriers to participation and pay community for their time. But we ran into a challenge: how can we bring community into an existing City tree planting request system that was not designed to prioritize community planting? George, from the Mayor's Office figured, if we just add some specific community codes when making requests, the city's foresters would recognize the submission and email of the community organizations that were approved in advance as a priority, and the City would plant their tree requests first. Then the City foresters taught us what it takes to survey the parkway to plant a tree so I can develop a community training program. We made the request process efficient and easy to learn (I coined in the training *"Look Up, Look Down, Look Around"* to help survey tree planting sites) which also built capacity for the City. As George used to say, we "deputized" residents to go out into the community, survey the parkway to assess tree planting suitability, go door-to-door in their neighborhoods that needed trees, discuss tree benefits with their neighbors, and pay them for their time to build the canopy in their neighborhood. We had limited funding, so I chose two communities that I felt had the drive to pilot this program, North Lawndale and South Lawndale, in 2022. It worked!

This became the [Tree Ambassador Program](https://mortonarb.org/plant-and-protect/chicago-region-trees-initiative/tree-ambassador-program) (TAP). By working with communities to develop the necessary training and support to get public trees accepted by residents and planted in their neighborhoods, in partnership with the Morton Arboretum's Chicago Region Trees Initiative. In 2023, Accenture helped us pro bono to take the TAP from pilot to program. We got earmark funding from US Representative Sean Casten to fund seven more community organizations. That is when Lydia and I applied for IRA funding from the USFS and we won funding for 50 more community organizations!

## RESULTS
*Details of metrics and results will be outlined soon, but by the end of 2022, the first year of the program, we achieved a net positive tree plantings vs tree loss for the first time in 10 years. This continues today. Also, 311 tree planting requests shifted from the northside to the west and south sides for the first time, with majority of tree plantings occurring in North Lawndale and other communities for the first time. It has also been studied that trees requested by TAP members survived over the two-year warranty of the tree vs non-TAP requested trees - showing that TAP-driven community-requested trees are invested in caring for those trees. A peer reviewed paper is forthcoming on mortality and Chicago's canopy - stay tuned.*


## FINAL THOUGHTS
The benefit of trees are multifold and include: promoting and improving physical and mental health, improving social cohesion, reducing stress, increasing property values, reducing energy bills, mitigating climate change, reducing urban heat islands and increasing cooling effects, decreasing soil erosion, storm water management and flood prevention, reducing air pollution, repairing local tree biodiversity, providing critical bird, small animal and essential insect habitats, amongst many other environmental benefits.

Trees also help prevent crime and the City of Chicago created [The Ripple Effect](https://montom.com/ripple-effect) campaign to raise awareness about the root causes of gun violence and how community-led cleaning and greening of vacant lots decrease violent crime by 40%. The myth that trees are related to crime has never been proven and was started in Los Angeles in the early 2000's and has been contorted in few variations across the nation over the years. This is documented in the article [Shade in the Places Journal](https://placesjournal.org/article/shade-an-urban-design-mandate), where, *“Requests to deforest are common in heavily policed areas, where shade is perceived as a magnet for drug dealing and prostitution...It’s not that the police have the authority to say, you can’t plant trees here. It’s that they have convinced community leaders that, if you want to save your community, you can’t have too many trees, because it restricts (the police’s) ability to do their jobs.”* The same arguments for gentrification also often occur and also have not been proven. Quoting a community member that was also a Tree Ambassador, *"It's not gentrification if it's done by us. We deserve the same benefits that other neighborhoods get."*

We summarized some of the benefits in this video by Bloomberg Associates who greatly helped us manage Our Roots Chicago for the first two years with us, [*"Why Tree Planting is Necessary for a Healthy Economy and Environment."*](https://youtu.be/C2BaoUcfjQc?si=md8VFrboelUozFIH)

As you can see, there are a multitude of community organizations, government departments and agencies, academics, and NGOs involved. Basically, a three-legged stool concept defined success - the need to work together from the start with each leg of community, government and researchers. So many people to thank and so many residents trusted us when they had no reason to. We visited so many Chicago neighborhoods. We went to their events, visited their homes, walked their blocks, went to their schools and churches, visited with their neighbors, broke bread with them and got to know each other. Every neighborhood was unique and so were the residents and we celebrated that diversity. We also visited researchers and organizations in person, taking tours of their work in neighborhoods. I took these learnings and relationships to other projects like the extreme heat work with [Defusing Disasters](https://buffett.northwestern.edu/research/global-working-groups/defusing-disasters.html) and the [Community Air Monitoring Network](https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/depts/cdph/CDPH/admin/program-summaries/sep/CDPH_OnePager_AirQuality_FINAL_7_7_23.pdf) - with some of the very same communities and partners, with the same goals of improving the health and well-being for all people but prioritizing neighborhoods that have suffered the most from racism, historic redlining, and under/dis-investment so that everyone gets the health and climate co-benefits.

Everyone has the right to access nature and its health benefits—and to thrive—just as nature and animals require within healthy ecosystems. We brought nature right to thousands of people's homes through Our Roots Chicago - to directly see and feel the benefits of having nature outside their front door. Someday, we won't be able to tell where nature ends and the city begins, it will become one.
